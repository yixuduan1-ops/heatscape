Method & interpretation

What building scale means

B is the percentile rank of the average of relative height and relative footprint area. It recognises larger-scale candidates for further project investigation. It is a policy preference for scale, not proof of more households, greater energy hardship or greater savings.

Taller buildings can have different shading, exposure and delivery challenges. A larger footprint can indicate more roof area, but usable roof area, dwelling counts and suitability need verification. No floors indicator is used.

Why height alone cannot establish thermal vulnerability

1. Compare like with like. Heat (H) and community low-income share (I) are converted into percentiles using the fixed study sample: (average ascending rank − 0.5) ÷ N × 100. Tied values share a rank. Height and footprint area are ranked in the same way; B is the percentile of their average. A high percentile means higher relative evidence, not a measured level of risk.

2. Apply your policy balance. Score = H × Heat share + I × Income share + B × Building share. Percentile values run from 0 to 100; shares are fractions totalling 1. Default: 40% / 40% / 20%. For H=80, I=60, B=50, the score is 80×0.4 + 60×0.4 + 50×0.2 = 66. All buildings use the same shares. Reset restores this balance.

3. Choose the shortlist. Rank all scored candidates from high to low. Top percentage selects the highest ceil(N × percentage /100) positions; Top number selects the requested count, capped at the eligible sample. Exact score ties at the cutoff are included, so the final count can be larger. The purple plane is this cutoff, not a weight surface or funding threshold. Filters never recalculate reference distributions or ranks.

Building scale ranks the mean of height and footprint percentiles. Floors and household counts are not included. Larger scale is a policy preference, not proof of need or savings.

The sampling region follows the whole footprint, excluding its interior. Partial raster cells contribute their actual overlap area; nearer areas receive greater distance influence. The overlay shows weighting, not temperature. Landsat's native thermal resolution is approximately 100 m, resampled to 30 m.

4. Sample surface heat. For each valid 30 m raster cell, its weight is the integral of exp(−2(d/R)²) over its actual overlap with the exterior footprint buffer. Here d is distance from the footprint and R is 50, 100 or 150 m. Temperature is the sum of cell temperature × weight divided by the sum of weights. Both valid-area and weighted coverage must reach 80%. ST_B10 × 0.00341802 + 149 − 273.15 converts to °C; fill and QA_PIXEL cloud, shadow, cirrus and snow bits are excluded. Mean ST_QA uncertainty is reported, not used as a further cutoff.

Income uses Census 2021 SA1 households below A$650/week divided by all households with known income, including negative/nil income. Partial or unstated income is excluded. A footprint interior point assigns the SA1; income is not measured for the building’s residents.

Three spatial checks compare 50 / 100 / 150 m, all using area and distance weighting with your current policy weights and shortlist size. The count shows how consistently a building is shortlisted across these extents; it is not a probability. Only checks with sufficient valid evidence are counted.

A high score does not mean this building is unsafe or eligible for funding. It means it is a higher-priority candidate for further investigation based on heat and neighbourhood socioeconomic context, together with the selected scale preference.

Evidence for targeted energy policy

HEATSCAPE is an independent student research prototype for the Victorian Energy Upgrades (VEU) program team and energy-policy decision-makers. It supports discussion of where building-focused energy-efficiency assistance, outreach or investigation might be prioritised; it is not an official VEU service.

Use the shortlist to identify candidates, compare how policy priorities change the ranking, and commission building-level checks before designing support. The score does not estimate energy savings, costs, retrofit feasibility or the value of a subsidy.

One policy, one set of weights

H is surrounding heat percentile; I is low-income community percentile; B is building-scale percentile. VEU policy users can explore a common balance for all candidates using one three-part policy slider. The default is 40% Heat, 40% Income and 20% Building.

The score is the sum of three weighted indicators. The H–I–B scatter shows the data and current shortlist cutoff. Larger buildings do not necessarily have greater need: scale is a transparent policy choice requiring verification.

What the evidence can tell us

Heat is single-date land surface temperature from Landsat 9 on 7 January 2026. It is not air or indoor temperature. The thermal sensor’s native resolution is approximately 100 m; the product is resampled to a 30 m grid. Neighbouring grid cells are not independent 30 m measurements. Area weighting makes the sampling boundary more precise, not the temperature of each building or roof.

Income is the share of households earning below A$650 per week among households with known income in the local 2021 SA1. It describes the neighbourhood, not the residents of an individual building.

Buildings remain apartment candidates. Data checks and source-reported heights are not a substitute for individual building verification.

Before any policy or funding decision, verify building use, resident needs, equipment and energy performance, ownership or owners-corporation arrangements, intervention costs and applicable program requirements.
