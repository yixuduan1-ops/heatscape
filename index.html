Method & interpretation

What building scale means

B is the percentile rank of the average of relative height and relative footprint area. It recognises larger-scale candidates for further project investigation. It is a policy preference for scale, not proof of more households, greater energy hardship or greater savings.

Taller buildings can have different shading, exposure and delivery challenges. A larger footprint can indicate more roof area, but usable roof area, dwelling counts and suitability need verification. No floors indicator is used.

Why height alone cannot establish thermal vulnerability

1. Compare like with like. Heat (H) and community low-income share (I) are converted into percentiles using the fixed study sample: (average ascending rank − 0.5) ÷ N × 100. Tied values share a rank. Height and footprint area are ranked in the same way; B is the percentile of their average. A high percentile means higher relative evidence, not a measured level of risk.

2. Apply your policy balance. Score = H × Heat share + I × Income share + B × Building share. Percentile values run from 0 to 100; shares are fractions totalling 1. Default: 40% / 40% / 20%. For H=80, I=60, B=50, the score is 80×0.4 + 60×0.4 + 50×0.2 = 66. All buildings use the same shares. Reset restores this balance.

3. Choose the shortlist. Rank all scored candidates from high to low. Top percentage selects the highest ceil(N × percentage /100) positions; Top number selects the requested count, capped at the eligible sample. Exact score ties at the cutoff are included, so the final count can be larger. The purple plane is this cutoff, not a weight surface or funding threshold. Filters never recalculate reference distributions or ranks.

Building scale ranks the mean of height and footprint percentiles. Floors and household counts are not included. Larger scale is a policy preference, not proof of need or savings.

Original sampling follows the whole footprint, excluding its interior. With Use sun-exposed H enabled, all mapped building footprints and observation-time shadows are additionally removed. Partial raster cells contribute their actual overlap area; nearer areas receive greater distance influence. The overlay shows weighting, not temperature. Landsat's native thermal resolution is approximately 100 m, resampled to 30 m.

4. Sample surface heat. For each valid 30 m raster cell, its weight is the integral of exp(−2(d/R)²) over its actual overlap with the exterior footprint buffer. Here d is distance from the footprint and R is 50, 100 or 150 m. Temperature is the sum of cell temperature × weight divided by the sum of weights. Both valid-area and weighted coverage must reach 80%. ST_B10 × 0.00341802 + 149 − 273.15 converts to °C; fill and QA_PIXEL cloud, shadow, cirrus and snow bits are excluded. Mean ST_QA uncertainty is reported, not used as a further cutoff.

Income uses Census 2021 SA1 households below A$650/week divided by all households with known income, including negative/nil income. Partial or unstated income is excluded. A footprint interior point assigns the SA1; income is not measured for the building’s residents.

The evidence behind the map

Public spatial datasets are combined to compare apartment candidates. Dates, spatial scales and limitations differ between sources.

Study area & coverage

2,573 apartment candidates across Melbourne, Port Phillip, Maribyrnong, Stonnington and Yarra. The default settings score 2,571 candidates with sufficient evidence. This is a research sample, not a complete verified register of apartments.

Building shade context · experimental geometry

Enable Building shadows in Priorities, then select a building. The outer 50 / 100 / 150 m distances are retained; sun-exposed H removes building footprints and shadows from these buffers. Geometry uses the scene metadata: solar elevation 56.65410279°, azimuth 73.04185990°. Shadows point opposite the sun; level-ground reach = building height / tan(solar elevation).

The added analysis separates modelled shaded and unshaded open ground and samples the existing LST raster with area and distance weights. It does not estimate cooling, indoor temperatures or wall heat transfer, and only changes H and rankings when Use sun-exposed H is enabled. Flat ground, simplified building volumes and potentially estimated heights limit the result. Trees and occluders missing from the dataset are excluded. See the panel for coverage and caveats.

Heat (H) · Land surface temperature

Source: USGS Landsat 9, 7 January 2026, scene centre 00:03:46 UTC / 11:03:46 AEDT. Values are in °C and describe surface heat on one satellite observation date, not current weather, indoor temperature or air temperature.

The thermal sensor has approximately 100 m native resolution; the supplied raster has a resampled 30 m grid. Smaller grid cells do not provide independent 30 m temperature measurements.

Heat is sampled 50, 100 or 150 m outwards from the whole building footprint, excluding its interior. Actual pixel overlap area and distance both contribute to the weighted mean. Clouds and other invalid pixels are excluded; both area and weighted coverage must reach 80%.

Income (I) · Community context

Source: ABS Census 2021, at Statistical Area Level 1 (SA1). The value is the percentage of households earning below A$650 per week among households with known income. Negative and nil income are included; partial or unstated income is excluded.

A point inside each building footprint assigns its SA1. This describes the surrounding community, not the income of individual residents. Census household totals belong to an area, not to each building.

ABS Census data ↗

Buildings (B) · Footprints & height

Sources: the study’s building dataset combines Overture Maps and City of Melbourne building information. Footprints describe building outlines and area (m²); height is in metres. Some heights are estimated or derived rather than directly measured. These datasets are snapshots, not live records.

B combines relative height and footprint area into a building-scale percentile. Larger scale represents potential policy reach, not verified household numbers, energy demand or retrofit savings. Household counts are not sufficiently complete at building level to be included. Floors are not a separate scoring indicator.

Apartment classifications, geometry and heights require verification. The optional grey buildings provide context only and are not ranked. Roof details, facades and terrain are not modelled.

Suburb, SA1, SA2 & council boundaries

Source: ABS Australian Statistical Geography Standard (ASGS), 2021, for suburb/locality and SA2 search boundaries.

Suburb: ABS Suburbs and Localities (SAL) approximate recognised suburbs and localities. Search a name such as Carlton North to outline the area. Building suburb labels use a point inside the footprint.

SA1: the smaller statistical area supplying the income indicator. Search its code to find matching buildings.

SA2: a larger statistical area used for neighbourhood search and orientation. Search its name or code to outline its boundary. It does not replace SA1 in the income calculation.

Council: the five local-government areas define study coverage and shortlist filters. A searched suburb or SA2 may extend beyond them; highlighting the full boundary does not extend candidate coverage or change rankings.

From data to priority

H, I and B are relative percentiles, not physical measurements of energy need. The same policy weights apply to every scored building (default H 40%, I 40%, B 20%). Missing evidence is not treated as zero need.

Evidence for targeted energy policy

HEATSCAPE is an independent student research prototype for the Victorian Energy Upgrades (VEU) program team and energy-policy decision-makers. It supports discussion of where building-focused energy-efficiency assistance, outreach or investigation might be prioritised; it is not an official VEU service.

Use the shortlist to identify candidates, compare how policy priorities change the ranking, and commission building-level checks before designing support. The score does not estimate energy savings, costs, retrofit feasibility or the value of a subsidy.

One policy, one set of weights

H is surrounding heat percentile; I is low-income community percentile; B is building-scale percentile. VEU policy users can explore a common balance for all candidates using one three-part policy slider. The default is 40% Heat, 40% Income and 20% Building.

The score is the sum of three weighted indicators. The H–I–B scatter shows the data and current shortlist cutoff. Larger buildings do not necessarily have greater need: scale is a transparent policy choice requiring verification.

What the evidence can tell us

Heat is single-date land surface temperature from Landsat 9 on 7 January 2026. It is not air or indoor temperature. The thermal sensor’s native resolution is approximately 100 m; the product is resampled to a 30 m grid. Neighbouring grid cells are not independent 30 m measurements. Area weighting makes the sampling boundary more precise, not the temperature of each building or roof.

Income is the share of households earning below A$650 per week among households with known income in the local 2021 SA1. It describes the neighbourhood, not the residents of an individual building.

Buildings remain apartment candidates. Data checks and source-reported heights are not a substitute for individual building verification.

Before any policy or funding decision, verify building use, resident needs, equipment and energy performance, ownership or owners-corporation arrangements, intervention costs and applicable program requirements.
