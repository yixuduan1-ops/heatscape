Method & interpretation

What building scale means

B is the percentile rank of the average of relative height and relative footprint area. It recognises larger-scale candidates for further project investigation. It is a policy preference for scale, not proof of more households, greater energy hardship or greater savings.

Taller buildings can have different shading, exposure and delivery challenges. A larger footprint can indicate more roof area, but usable roof area, dwelling counts and suitability need verification. No floors indicator is used.

Why height alone cannot establish thermal vulnerability

1. Put inputs on a common scale. Within the fixed reference population, each available heat-temperature, income, rent-ratio, height or area input becomes a percentile: (average ascending rank − 0.5) ÷ N × 100. Tied values share a rank. H and I then combine their components using the trees above; B is the percentile of the mean of height and area percentiles. These scores express relative screening evidence, not measured risk.

2. Apply your policy balance. Score = H × Heat share + I × Economic-pressure share + B × Building share. H, I and B values run from 0 to 100; shares are fractions totalling 1. Default: 40% / 40% / 20%. For H=80, I=60, B=50, the score is 80×0.4 + 60×0.4 + 50×0.2 = 66. All buildings use the same shares. Reset restores this balance.

3. Choose the shortlist. Rank all scored candidates from high to low. Top percentage selects the highest ceil(N × percentage /100) positions; Top number selects the requested count, capped at the eligible sample. Exact score ties at the cutoff are included, so the final count can be larger. The purple plane is this cutoff, not a weight surface or funding threshold. Filters never recalculate reference distributions or ranks.

Building scale ranks the mean of height and footprint percentiles. Floors and household counts are not included. Larger scale is a policy preference, not proof of need or savings.

4. Sample surface heat. Each Landsat observation uses a footprint buffer minus mapped buildings and date-specific ground shadows. A 2.5 m numerical grid approximates the area integral of exp(−2(d/R)²), where d is distance from the footprint and R is 50, 100 or 150 m. Temperature is the weighted sum of valid source-cell temperatures divided by valid weights. Both valid area and weight must reach 80%, with at least 3 valid resampled cells. ST_B10 × 0.00341802 + 149 − 273.15 converts to °C; fill, dilated cloud, cloud, cloud shadow, cirrus and snow are excluded. ST_QA uncertainty is reported separately.

The fixed multi-date input takes the median of valid per-date statistics and requires at least 3 dates. H combines its percentile with a historical temperature branch using adjustable internal shares (initially 70/30). The historical index is the equal mean of CSIRO UHI, Victoria UHI and summer climate percentiles. All blends are exploratory assumptions. See About data for every source, period, file and coverage count.

The low-income component uses Census 2021 SA1 households below A$650/week divided by all households with known income, including negative/nil income. Partial or unstated income is excluded. A footprint interior point assigns the SA1; income is not measured for the building’s residents. I combines low-income-share percentile with the percentile of same-year SA1 median rent / median household income; internal shares are adjustable (initially 70/30). This is a ratio of area medians, not measured household rental stress. CLUE use and age remain separate evidence.

The evidence behind the map

Study area & coverage

2,573 apartment candidates across Melbourne, Port Phillip, Maribyrnong, Stonnington and Yarra. The current eligible count appears in Priorities; incomplete income or heat/climate evidence prevents a full score. This is a research sample, not a complete verified register of apartments.

Economic pressure (I) · shared SA1 context

Source: ABS Census 2021, at Statistical Area Level 1 (SA1). The value is the percentage of households earning below A$650 per week among households with known income. Negative and nil income are included; partial or unstated income is excluded.

A point inside each building footprint assigns its SA1. This describes the surrounding community, not the income of individual residents. Census household totals belong to an area, not to each building. The I tree adds a 30% component from the percentile of the 2021 SA1 median rent / median household income ratio. That ratio is not actual household rental stress; age and building type are separate evidence.

Buildings (B) · Footprints & height

Sources: the study’s building dataset combines Overture Maps and City of Melbourne building information. Footprints describe building outlines and area (m²); height is in metres. Some heights are estimated or derived rather than directly measured. These datasets are snapshots, not live records.

B combines relative height and footprint area into a building-scale percentile. Larger scale represents potential policy reach, not verified household numbers, energy demand or retrofit savings. Household counts are not sufficiently complete at building level to be included. Floors are not a separate scoring indicator.

Apartment classifications, geometry and heights require verification. The optional grey buildings provide context only and are not ranked. Roof details, facades and terrain are not modelled.

Suburb, SA1, SA2 & council boundaries

Source: ABS Australian Statistical Geography Standard (ASGS), 2021, for suburb/locality and SA2 search boundaries.

Suburb: ABS Suburbs and Localities (SAL) approximate recognised suburbs and localities. Search a name such as Carlton North to outline the area. Building suburb labels use a point inside the footprint.

SA1: the smaller statistical area supplying the income indicator. Search its code to find matching buildings.

SA2: a larger statistical area used for neighbourhood search and orientation. Search its name or code to outline its boundary. It does not replace SA1 in the income calculation.

Council: the five local-government areas define study coverage and shortlist filters. A searched suburb or SA2 may extend beyond them; highlighting the full boundary does not extend candidate coverage or change rankings.

About the Victorian Energy Upgrades program

A guide to prioritisation

A high score does not mean this building is unsafe or eligible for funding. It means it is a higher-priority candidate for further investigation based on heat and neighbourhood socioeconomic context.

One policy, one set of weights

H combines multi-day surface heat with a historical temperature branch; I combines low-income share with an area rent-to-income proxy; B is building-scale percentile. VEU policy users can explore a common balance for all candidates using the outer three-part slider and separate internal H and I sliders. The default is 40% Heat, 40% Economic pressure and 20% Building.

The score is the sum of three weighted indicators. The H–I–B scatter shows the data and current shortlist cutoff. Larger buildings do not necessarily have greater need: scale is a transparent policy choice requiring verification.

What the evidence can tell us

Heat uses one fixed composite: a multi-date Landsat median plus historical UHI and Climate Victoria summer normals, normalised before combining. The supplied dates are snapshots, not a complete seasonal record. It is not air or indoor temperature. The thermal sensor’s native resolution is approximately 100 m; the product is resampled to a 30 m grid. Neighbouring grid cells are not independent 30 m measurements. Area weighting makes the sampling boundary more precise, not the temperature of each building or roof.

I combines low-income-share and same-year area rent-to-income percentiles. Internal shares are adjustable, initially 70/30. These both describe the 2021 SA1, not individual residents. CLUE use/age and 2025 rental benchmarks are displayed separately.

Buildings remain apartment candidates. Data checks and source-reported heights are not a substitute for individual building verification.

Before any policy or funding decision, verify building use, resident needs, equipment and energy performance, ownership or owners-corporation arrangements, intervention costs and applicable program requirements.
